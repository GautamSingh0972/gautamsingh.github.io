There’s More to the Pretzel: The Hidden Economics of Wetzel’s Clustering Strategy

By Gautam Singh · Economics Blog · Based on Planet Money’s reporting on Wetzel’s Pretzels

At first glance, the Atlantic Avenue story in “There’s No Business Like Dough Business” looks like a simple story about putting more pretzel kiosks where people walk past. But underneath the dough, lemonade and frozen drinks is a much more interesting economic problem: how many sales can one production location capture before the cost of adding another outlet outweighs the benefit?

Ricky’s strategy works because several pieces of the business fit together unusually well. Tiny kiosks can share production with a larger bakery, multiple locations can capture different passenger flows, and an existing franchisee can spread its infrastructure and management costs across several sales points.

But the episode leaves several important questions unanswered. What does the rent actually look like? How much sales does a second location steal from the first? How far can one commissary realistically serve? Does Wetzel’s corporate have an incentive to encourage clustering? And, perhaps most importantly, was Ricky buying profitable locations or buying the right to keep competitors out?

First, how is rent actually priced in a transit hub?

The episode does not explain whether the kiosk rents are fixed, based on a percentage of revenue, or negotiated as part of a wider multi-unit agreement. Yet the “all-or-nothing” landlord offer hints at a much more complicated lease structure than an ordinary monthly rent cheque.

MAG versus percentage rent

A common structure in high-traffic retail is the greater of a fixed minimum rent or a percentage of sales. The Minimum Annual Guarantee (MAG) gives the landlord a guaranteed floor, while percentage rent lets the landlord participate when the tenant performs exceptionally well.

The point at which the two become equal is the natural breakpoint. If annual MAG were $72,000 and percentage rent were 10%, the breakpoint would be $720,000 in annual sales. Below that level, the tenant would pay the MAG; above it, the percentage calculation would become more expensive.

This structure is attractive to a landlord because it combines protection with upside. A weak location still produces a minimum payment, while a high-performing location allows the landlord to capture some of the value created by unusually strong foot traffic.

The all-or-nothing problem

The landlord’s decision to bundle multiple locations changes the economics again. One highly productive kiosk can effectively subsidise a weaker one. The landlord gets one tenant, one agreement and rent across the whole portfolio, while the franchisee takes on the operational risk of making every location work.

That arrangement can also reduce vacancies and prevent a competitor from taking one of the better spaces. If percentage-rent calculations are aggregated across the locations, it can further prevent revenue from being shifted between stores simply to avoid crossing an individual sales breakpoint.

The tenant-improvement trap

There is another asymmetry: the tenant may have to pay for the infrastructure required to make a raw transit space usable. Plumbing, heavy electrical work, ventilation and other build-out costs can require a substantial upfront investment.

If permanent improvements remain with the property when the lease ends, the franchisee takes on another risk. It may spend heavily to create a functioning food outlet and then have to leave that investment behind if the lease is not renewed.

So how much does one Wetzel’s cannibalise another?

This is one of the most important questions the episode leaves open. Fischer describes cannibalization as “a little bit, but not very much,” but there is no company-wide measured rate provided.

The only concrete figure is Ricky’s Sherman Oaks experience. When he opened a second Wetzel’s in the same mall, the original store initially experienced a 10–15% decline in sales. The surprising part is what happened next: the original store recovered its previous sales level within six months while the new store continued generating its own revenue.

That distinction matters. The 10–15% figure should not be treated as Wetzel’s general cannibalization rate. It is a single case study of a temporary decline following an expansion.

In fact, the recovery creates an even more interesting possibility. Some of the initial decline may simply have been customers changing which Wetzel’s they used, while the second location also captured impulse purchases that would otherwise have been missed altogether.

The real unanswered question is therefore not “What is Wetzel’s cannibalization rate?” but “How much of the sales lost by an existing store is permanently transferred to the new store, and how much new demand is created by increased visibility?”

Does the commissary model actually scale?

The Atlantic Avenue arrangement is unusually compact. Ricky can supply the satellite locations from the bakery upstairs, keeping production labour centralised while allowing each small outlet to operate with very little staff.

That does not prove a commissary can serve kiosks over an arbitrarily large radius. The economics are better understood as a balance between two forces:

Kitchen and labour savings > delivery, labour, spoilage, freshness and coordination costs.

As distance increases, someone has to make the delivery whether the order contains 20 pretzels or 200. Travel time increases, freshness becomes harder to manage, and eventually dedicated vehicles, drivers, holding capacity and more sophisticated routing may be necessary.

Wetzel’s history of using small satellite kiosks supplied by nearby standard stores supports the idea that proximity matters. At the same time, the normal franchise model remains largely decentralised, with individual bakeries handling their own fresh preparation.

That suggests the commissary is best understood as a density strategy, not a distance strategy.

The better question is therefore:

How many satellite locations does one production kitchen need before the incremental delivery and logistics costs outweigh the savings from centralised production?

Who actually benefits from clustering?

Wetzel’s current franchise economics create another interesting incentive. The standard franchise model includes a 7% royalty fee and a 1% advertising-fund contribution, both tied to revenue.

That means corporate benefits directly when an existing franchisee adds another productive sales channel. If the additional kiosk produces incremental revenue, Wetzel’s receives additional royalty income even if the original store loses some sales.

Three different incentives

  • Wetzel’s corporate cares primarily about total system revenue and the royalties generated from it.
  • The multi-unit franchisee cares about combined profit across all locations.
  • The individual store cares about its own sales and profitability.

These incentives can overlap, but they are not identical. Corporate could benefit from a second location even when it cannibalises the first, provided total revenue increases.

But royalty revenue is not profit. The franchisee still has to pay rent, labour, food costs, logistics, advertising contributions, royalties and the initial build-out. The new location only makes sense if the incremental profit after all of those costs is attractive.

This is where shared production becomes powerful. If the satellite requires very little additional kitchen infrastructure, inventory or labour, the incremental economics can become much better.

The deeper question is therefore whether Wetzel’s royalty structure gives corporate an incentive to encourage clustering even when an individual store experiences cannibalization.

Was Ricky buying sales or buying out the competition?

The landlord’s all-or-nothing offer creates another possible explanation for Ricky taking the extra spaces: defensive clustering.

There are two fundamentally different reasons to take another kiosk:

  • Offensive: the new location generates profitable incremental sales.
  • Defensive: controlling the space prevents another food concept from accessing the same commuter traffic.

The second possibility is particularly interesting because the competitor would not need to sell pretzels. Coffee, doughnuts, pizza, sandwiches, smoothies, ice cream or another impulse-oriented food concept could all compete for the same passing customers.

Ricky’s broader philosophy is essentially about “bringing the pretzels to the people.” If that is the business model, controlling strategically visible points of pedestrian traffic could have value beyond the direct sales produced by each kiosk.

The missing counterfactual

But there is no evidence in the episode identifying a specific competing bidder. The real question is what would have happened if Ricky had said no.

  • If a competitor would have taken the space: the decision has a genuine defensive component.
  • If the space would have remained vacant: the decision is primarily about incremental Wetzel’s sales.
  • If Wetzel’s corporate encouraged control of the spaces: clustering may be an intentional competitive real-estate strategy.

There is even a counterargument: another food tenant might increase the attractiveness of the area and generate more overall food traffic rather than simply taking customers away.

So the missing question is:

What was the landlord’s next-best use for those kiosk spaces, and how would that alternative have affected Ricky’s existing Wetzel’s locations?

What does the post-pandemic commuter economy change?

The final unanswered question is much bigger than Atlantic Avenue. Ricky’s original store was still being compared with its stronger 2018–19 performance, but the episode does not establish whether the specific kind of foot traffic Wetzel’s needs has fully recovered.

“Commuters came back” is too simple. Different types of traffic have behaved differently:

  • Office commuters: structurally weaker because of hybrid work.
  • Transit riders: potentially more resilient depending on the station.
  • Leisure and event traffic: potentially strong, particularly around major entertainment venues.
  • Weekend traffic: less affected by remote work.
  • Impulse purchases: potentially strong if the remaining pedestrians are concentrated in high-quality locations.

That makes Atlantic Avenue potentially unusual. It is not simply an office-worker corridor; it sits within a dense combination of transit, retail, leisure and entertainment activity.

Wetzel’s franchise data provide some evidence that non-traditional and transit locations continue to generate meaningful revenue, while the company's reported figures for locations using Remote Mobile Units are consistent with the idea that adding small satellite sales points can increase the revenue captured by an existing production location.

But there is a major selection-bias problem. Atlantic Avenue may combine unusually high transit density, entertainment traffic, multiple pedestrian flows, cheap shared production and an experienced multi-unit operator. Its success therefore tells us much less about an ordinary commuter corridor whose weekday traffic may have permanently fallen.

The post-pandemic market may be splitting in two

The best high-density locations may actually have become more valuable, while mediocre commuter locations have become less attractive.

If that is true, Wetzel’s does not need 2019’s total number of pedestrians everywhere. It needs to put its product directly in front of the pedestrians who are still there and most likely to buy.

That leads to the biggest macro question left unanswered by the episode:

Has the decline in routine commuting made the “put a pretzel wherever people pass” strategy more valuable because remaining foot traffic is concentrated, or less valuable because the overall pool of impulse customers has permanently shrunk?

The takeaway

The Atlantic Avenue story looks, on the surface, like a story about pretzels. Economically, it is a story about density.

The strategy works when several conditions line up: valuable foot traffic, strategically positioned real estate, shared production, low incremental labour costs and enough sales volume to justify the additional locations.

But that also explains why the episode should not be read as proof that “more kiosks” is always better. The economics depend on the lease, the degree of cannibalization, the distance from the production kitchen, the incentives created by the franchise agreement, the competitive alternative for the space and the quality of the underlying foot traffic.

In other words, the real business is not simply selling pretzels. It is converting expensive, high-density foot traffic into revenue while minimising the incremental cost of reaching each additional customer.

Atlantic Avenue is compelling precisely because all of those variables appear to reinforce one another. The unanswered question is whether that makes Ricky’s strategy a clever one-off—or a repeatable model for the future of fast-casual food retail.

Key Terms

Minimum Annual Guarantee (MAG) — a minimum amount of rent a tenant must pay regardless of sales.

Percentage rent — rent calculated as a percentage of a tenant’s sales, often used alongside a minimum rent.

Natural breakpoint — the sales level at which percentage rent equals the fixed minimum rent.

Cannibalization — the reduction in sales at an existing location caused by opening another location that serves some of the same customers.

Commissary — a central production kitchen that prepares products or ingredients for multiple smaller sales locations.

Defensive clustering — occupying multiple nearby locations partly to prevent competitors from gaining access to strategically valuable customer traffic.
